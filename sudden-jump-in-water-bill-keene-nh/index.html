Sudden Jump in Your Water Bill: Finding a Hidden Leak in Keene, NH

A bill that climbs without a change in habits often means water is going somewhere you cannot see. A fifteen-minute meter test can tell you whether the leak is inside the house, and where to look first.

Start with the meter

Shut off every faucet and appliance. Find your water meter, usually in the basement near the street side or in a pit outside. Look for a small dial or a triangle that spins when water moves. If it turns with everything off, water is flowing through a leak. Write the number, wait an hour without using water and read it again.

If the number changed, the leak is real. If the meter stays still, the high bill may come from a billing error, a rate change or a seasonal use such as filling a pool or watering a lawn.

When the leak is inside a wall or under a slab

Sometimes the meter turns and you find no wet spot. Water may be leaking inside a wall, under a floor or beneath a slab. Warm spots on a floor, a damp smell, cracked paint or the sound of running water at night point to it. A plumber can use acoustic listening gear or pressure tests to locate the leak. A thermal camera and moisture meter map the damage.

Slab leaks need particular care. See our slab leak page for signs. For leaks in walls, see our wall and drywall page.

What to do when you find it

  1. Close the nearest shutoff, or the main valve, to stop the flow.
  2. Take photos of the damage and the leak source.
  3. Call a plumber to fix the line or fixture.
  4. Call a restoration company if walls, floors or ceilings are wet.
  5. Call your utility to ask about leak adjustment policies.

Some utilities offer a billing adjustment after a documented leak. Ask yours, and keep the plumber's invoice and your meter readings.

Reading the bill itself

Look at the usage line, not just the total. Compare this period with the same months last year, because summer use can run higher for lawns and pools. Check that the meter reading was actual and not an estimate. If your bill is based on an estimate, a corrected reading can change the number. Call the utility if the numbers do not make sense.

Leak sensors and shutoffs

Small battery-powered sensors that beep when they touch water are inexpensive and useful under sinks, behind toilets, beside water heaters and next to washers. Some smart shutoff valves close the main supply when they detect a leak. Whether you add one or not, know where the main valve is, test it once a year and label it for everyone in the house.

Why small leaks add up

A toilet flapper that does not seal, a faucet that drips and a pinhole in a pipe can each run all day and all night. Over a month, even a slow leak can account for a large share of the bill. The same leaks soak materials slowly, which is how a wall or floor can be ruined without a single dramatic event.

What to tell the plumber

  • When the bill went up and by how much.
  • Whether the meter turns with everything off.
  • Any wet spots, warm floors or noises you have noticed.
  • Whether you have a slab, crawl space or basement.
  • Whether you have already checked the toilets.

Seasonal bill changes versus leaks

Not every jump is a leak. Filling a pool, watering a new lawn, a houseguest stretch or a change in billing period can raise usage. A leak looks different. Usage stays high every day, including at night and when nobody is home. Compare daily readings over a few days if your utility provides them, or read the meter yourself in the morning and the evening.

A leak between the meter and the house

A broken service line can leak outside, where you never see it, and soaked ground or a soft spot in the lawn may be the only sign. Ask your utility who is responsible for the pipe between the meter and the house. A plumber can test it and tell you whether it needs repair.

Public water, private wells and your bill

Not every home near Keene has a water bill. State municipal profiles list public suppliers such as Marlborough Water Works for Marlborough and Walpole Water for Walpole, while homes in Westmoreland rely on private wells. A well owner has no bill to show a leak.

Instead, watch for a pump that runs more often than usual, a pressure tank that cycles quickly or a higher electric bill. Those are the well owner's version of a spike.

More on sudden water bill jump

How much water can a running toilet waste?

Quite a lot over a month, though the amount varies by toilet. The dye test takes minutes.

Will my insurance cover the leak itself?

Policies often cover sudden damage but not the repair of the failed part. A gradual leak may be excluded. Ask your insurer.

Should I shut off the main water while I am away?

For a long absence in winter, many people do, especially in cold weather when pipes can freeze. Ask a plumber about draining lines.
