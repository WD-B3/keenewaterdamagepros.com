Where the East Side sits and how it grew

The City of Keene's own walking tour of downtown groups the Church Street neighborhood, the East Side neighborhood and the Beaver Brook corridor together on the east side of the city. Church Street was laid out in 1811 as Beaver Street, running from Main Street to Beaver Brook, and it grew from mills and businesses into housing for factory workers.

The city's 1925 zoning made Church Street business use from Main Street to Carpenter Street, general residential for one block, and single-family residential east of Carpenter. Today the same walk passes a former shoe factory turned into affordable housing, a senior housing building and apartments built on former railroad land. No official boundary for the East Side was found, so we describe it by those landmarks.

What the record says about flooding near Beaver Brook

According to the same city tour, major floods hit the Beaver Brook area in the 1950s, 1960s and 1970s. The Army Corps of Engineers built retaining walls along the brook between 1985 and 1987. Floods in 2005, 2007 and 2012 then caused significant damage to housing in the East Side neighborhood, and a Church Street pocket park now sits on a site of repetitive flood loss.

Keene's 2025 hazard mitigation plan adds that local rainstorms in the Beaver Brook watershed can cause significant flooding on the east side of the city. It also says the Ashuelot River and Beaver Brook floodplain carries very heavy residential and commercial development. We have not verified FEMA flood-zone status for the East Side as a whole, and zones differ from house to house, so search your own address in FEMA's map service center.

Not every East Side call involves the brook

Most water damage in any neighborhood starts indoors. A cracked supply line, a failed water heater, a leaking dishwasher hose or a roof leak can soak a unit and the ones below it. In a building with several apartments, water moves between floors and units, so the first call is often to the owner or property manager as well as to us.

Brook water and sewer water are a different category. Floodwater that comes in from outside, or a backup from a drain, can carry contamination, and it needs protective gear and careful removal instead of a mop and a fan. If you see water entering from outside, stay out of it and call before you touch anything electrical.

Brook flooding and your policy

Insurers treat rising brook water and a burst pipe as different events. The NH Insurance Department notes that homeowners policies generally leave out most flooding, so flood coverage is a separate policy. Sewer and drain backup is often an added endorsement. Read your declarations page or ask your agent. Coverage is theirs to decide, and our photos and moisture readings are there to support your records.

East Side water damage questions

Is the East Side in a FEMA flood zone?

Parts of the Beaver Brook corridor are in the mapped floodplain, but status changes from one property to the next. We cannot confirm a zone for your house. Search your address on FEMA's flood map service center or ask your insurer or the city's Community Development office.

Does brook water count as flood damage for insurance?

Water that rises from outside, such as a brook or river, is usually treated as flood and often needs a separate flood policy. A burst pipe inside is a different cause. Your insurer decides how your loss is classified.

Do you work on East Side apartment buildings?

Yes. Water in one unit often wets others, so we map moisture across floors and report to the owner. For larger buildings see commercial water damage.

What should I do first if brook water gets into my basement?

Stay out if water is near electrical equipment, keep children and pets away and call. Do not run a household vacuum or fan in the water. Our flood cleanup page lists the first steps in order.
