How to Prepare Your Keene, NH Home for Storm Season

Most storm water damage starts with something small: a clogged gutter, a sump pump that is not tested, a valve nobody can find. This checklist covers the checks that take an afternoon and may save a basement.

Know what happened here before

Local records show this is not hypothetical. Keene's housing resiliency analysis lists flood damage in 2005 and 2012, and Sullivan's town plan says a May 2012 storm washed out several roads. Heavy rain, rapid snowmelt and ice are normal risks around Cheshire County.

Outside the house

  • Clean gutters and downspouts, and extend downspouts several feet from the foundation.
  • Regrade low spots so soil slopes away from the house.
  • Check window wells for debris and drainage, and cover them if you can.
  • Look at roof flashing, chimneys and valleys for lifted or missing material.
  • Clear storm drains near your property of leaves and snow.

Check your insurance before the storm

Flood insurance is a separate purchase, and the state's regulator warns that a new policy may not pay for a loss in its first 30 days. Waiting until a storm is forecast is too late. Ask your agent about sewer and drain backup coverage, which is often an endorsement.

Check the FEMA flood map for your exact address, not just your town. Our flood damage page explains how flood losses differ from other water losses.

During the storm

  • Keep out of basements if water is near outlets, the panel or the furnace.
  • Do not pump a flooded basement all at once if the ground outside is saturated.
  • Photograph water levels and damage as it happens.
  • Call your insurer, then call a restoration company.

A one-afternoon plan

  1. Clean the gutters and check downspouts.
  2. Test the sump pump and check the discharge line outdoors.
  3. Locate and label the main water shutoff and the electrical panel.
  4. Move items off the basement floor and onto shelves.
  5. Read your policy and call your agent about flood and drain backup.

Power outages and pump backup

A sump pump does nothing without power. A battery backup or a water-powered backup pump can keep the pit from overflowing during an outage. Test the battery on a schedule. A generator can run a pump, but never run one indoors or in an attached garage. If you have no backup, plan to check the pit by flashlight during heavy rain.

Keep a simple kit

A flashlight, spare batteries, a phone charger, rubber boots, work gloves, a wet vacuum and a stack of old towels cover most small problems. Add a plastic tub for papers, a list of important phone numbers and a copy of your policy.

A five-minute walk after heavy rain

  • Look at the sump pit and listen for the pump.
  • Check the corners of the basement floor and the bottom of the walls.
  • Look at ceilings and the tops of exterior walls for new stains.
  • Walk the outside and check whether water pooled near the foundation.
  • Check the crawl space with a flashlight if you have one.

Catching a small leak this way is far easier than finding a large one later. Take a photo of anything unusual so you can compare it next time.

If you rent or live in a condo

Your landlord or association is responsible for much of the building, but you are responsible for your belongings and for telling them quickly about leaks. Ask what they have in place for roof, gutter and drainage care. Renter's insurance is worth having, because the building policy usually does not cover your things.

Plan an exit and a meeting point

If the basement floods or the power goes out, everyone in the house should know where the shutoffs are, which rooms to stay out of and who to call. Keep a flashlight near the stairs, write the numbers on the fridge and, if anyone has trouble with stairs, plan how they will move to a higher floor.

Storm access on rural roads

Hill roads in towns like Sullivan and Westmoreland can wash out or ice over, and driveways can be steep. If you call us during a storm, tell us about the road and the driveway so we can plan.

After the storm, look for roof leaks, wet insulation and water in crawl spaces before you assume the house came through fine.

After the storm

Walk the house with a flashlight. Look at ceilings, the tops of exterior walls, the basement floor and the crawl space. Check the sump pit. Note anything that smells damp. A small stain a week after a storm is easier to deal with than a wall that stays wet. See our storm cleanup page for the next steps.

More on storm season checklist

How often should I test the sump pump?

A few times a year and before every spring and fall storm season, by pouring water into the pit.

Does a backwater valve stop all sewer backups?

It helps prevent backup through floor drains, but a plumber can tell you what fits your home.

Can I buy flood insurance right before a storm?

A new flood policy may have a waiting period, so plan well ahead.

What should I do first if my basement floods?

Stay away from electrical hazards, stop the source if it is plumbing, and call. See our basement cleanup page.
